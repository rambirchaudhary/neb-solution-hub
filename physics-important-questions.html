Fluid Mechanics

  1. Define surface tension and state whether it depends on the surface area of a liquid. Show that the surface energy per unit area of a liquid is numerically equal to its surface tension. [1+1+3]
  2. State Pascal's law and explain why fluid pressure does not depend on the cross-sectional area of its container. For a cylindrical vessel, determine the height to which it must be filled with liquid so that the force exerted on its curved side equals the force exerted on its base. [1+2+2]
  3. State Pascal's law and derive the expression P = ρgh for fluid pressure at depth, where the symbols carry their usual meanings. A uniform tube has two vertical arms joined by a horizontal section 8 cm long at the bottom. Water filling 22 cm of one arm is poured in, and oil of specific gravity 0.8 filling 22 cm is poured into the other arm. Determine the position of the interface between the two liquids. [1+2+2]
  4. State Archimedes' principle and discuss whether it applies to floating bodies. An alloy made of copper (density 8.9 g/cm³) and zinc (density 7.1 g/cm³) weighs 12.9 g in air and 11.3 g when fully submerged in water. Calculate the mass fraction of each metal in the alloy. [1+1+3]
  5. State the law of floatation and comment on whether a floating body is in equilibrium. A wooden cube, loaded with a 200 g mass, just floats in water. When the mass is removed, the cube rises by 2 cm. Find the dimensions of the cube. [1+2+2]
  6. Define streamline flow and explain the conditions under which it becomes turbulent. An aircraft wing is to be designed to produce a lift of 1000 N per square metre of wing area. If air moves at 150 m/s beneath the wing, find the required air speed above the wing. (Take air density = 1.3 kg/m³) [1+1+3]
  7. Define the viscosity of a fluid and describe how it varies with temperature. An air bubble of radius 1 cm rises at a constant velocity of 3.5 mm/s through a liquid of density 1.75 × 10³ kg/m³. Calculate the coefficient of viscosity of the liquid, ignoring the density of air. [1+1+3]

Heat & Thermodynamics

  1. Explain the term thermodynamic variable and describe what makes up the internal energy of a system. Water falls from a height of 350 m; assuming all the energy of the fall converts into heat, calculate the rise in temperature. (Specific heat capacity of water = 4200 J/kg·K) [1+2+2]
  2. Define the molar heat capacity of a gas and explain why its value at constant pressure exceeds that at constant volume. Raising the temperature of 3 moles of an ideal gas from 25°C to 30°C at constant pressure requires 105 calories. Find the heat needed to produce the same temperature rise at constant volume. [1+2+2]
  3. Explain what is meant by an isothermal process and derive the expression for the work done when a gas expands from pressure P₁ to P₂ at constant temperature. 5 moles of gas at 1 atm and 20°C expand isothermally from 5 litres to 50 litres; calculate the work done by the gas. [1+2+2]
  4. Explain the significance of the first law of thermodynamics. At constant volume, the heat supplied equals the change in internal energy, but this relation does not hold at constant pressure — explain why. Two moles of an ideal gas are heated from 250 K to 500 K at constant pressure; sketch the P-V diagram for this process and find the work done. [1+2+1+1]
  5. Discuss the importance of the P-V diagram in thermodynamics. For the process shown involving 0.45 mol of an ideal gas, determine the temperatures at the two marked points, the work done during the process, and the heat required to raise the internal energy by 15000 J. [2+3]
  6. Explain the meaning of an adiabatic process. Starting from PVγ = constant, derive the expression for work done during an adiabatic process, W = (P₁V₁ − P₂V₂)/(γ − 1). During the adiabatic expansion of 0.5 mol of argon gas, the temperature drops from 520 K to 70 K; find the work done.
  7. State Kelvin's statement of the second law of thermodynamics, and give the efficiency expression for a Carnot engine running between two temperatures. A heat engine draws 500 calories from a source at 200°C and rejects 200 calories to a sink, giving it 75% efficiency. Find the sink temperature, and determine how much the sink temperature must be lowered to raise the efficiency to 85%. [1+1+3]
  8. Describe a petrol engine and explain why it requires a spark plug. Sketch the P-V diagram for the Otto cycle, and state how its efficiency relates to the compression ratio. [1+1+2+1]
  9. Identify which type of engine the accompanying P-V diagram represents and justify your answer. Explain what the marked straight line on the diagram signifies, and discuss whether this engine type needs a spark plug. [2+2+1]
  10. Define the coefficient of performance of a refrigerator, and explain why external work input is necessary to run one. A refrigerator absorbs 200 J of heat while 100 J of work is supplied to it; calculate its coefficient of performance. [1+2+2]
  11. Explain the concept of entropy and show how it arises from the first law of thermodynamics. Calculate the change in entropy when 1 kg of ice melts at 0°C. [1+2+2]
  12. Explain why the expansion stroke of an engine is referred to as the power stroke, and why diesel engines are built with a higher compression ratio. A petrol engine burns 5 kg of fuel per hour and delivers 20 kW of power; given the calorific value of petrol is 11 × 10³ kcal/kg, find the engine's efficiency. [1+1+3]

Magnetic Effect of Current

  1. Given that the magnetic force on a charged particle is F = q(v × B), show that a proton moving perpendicular to a magnetic field follows a circular path. Explain under what condition this proton could experience zero force. Find the force on a 5 m wire carrying a 2 A current in a 2 T magnetic field, given that the wire's cross-sectional area vector is parallel to the current. [2+2+1]
  2. Explain the right-hand thumb rule used in magnetism. Derive the expression F = q(v × B) for the magnetic force on a moving charge, and use it to define what constitutes a magnetic field of 1 tesla at a point. [1+3+1]
  3. State the right-hand palm rule for magnetic force. Explain how a magnetic force can produce torque on a particle while doing no work on it. An electron travels at 2 × 10⁶ m/s through a region of magnetic field 7.6 × 10⁻² T, entering at an unknown angle; find the maximum and minimum force it could experience. [1+2+2]
  4. Define the Lorentz force. Assess whether a lightning strike carrying current on the order of 10⁵ A could bend a metal flagpole, and explain the reasoning. A 3.00 cm square lies in the xy-plane at z = 0; find the magnitude of the magnetic flux through it due to the field B = 0.225î + 0.350ĵ − 0.500k̂. [1+2+2]
  5. A rectangular coil of length l and width b, carrying current I, sits in a uniform magnetic field B. a. Determine the force on each arm of the coil. [2] b. Can this force generate a torque on the coil? Support your answer with a calculation. [2] c. Explain the role this plays in the design of a moving-coil galvanometer. [2] d. Why is the magnetic field in a galvanometer designed to be radial? [2]
  6. Describe what happens when a current-carrying conductor is placed with its current perpendicular to an external magnetic field. A copper strip 2 mm thick and 2 cm wide carries 0.1 A current perpendicular to a 2 T field, producing a Hall voltage of 2 µV across its top and bottom faces. Determine the current density, the electric field, and the free electron concentration in the strip. [2+3]
  7. State the Biot-Savart law and mention the type of current it applies to. Apply it to derive the magnetic field at the centre of a current-carrying circular coil. A 500-turn coil carrying 0.2 A produces a field of 2.5 mT at its centre; find the coil's diameter. [1+2+2]
  8. State Ampere's circuital law and use it to derive the magnetic field at the centre of a solenoid of length l with n turns per unit length, each carrying current I. An MRI scanner's coil has 5 million turns of insulated copper wire wound on a 0.5 m radius; if a current of 7.25 A flows through it, find the magnetic field experienced by a patient positioned at its centre.
  9. Define the ampere in terms of magnetic force between conductors. Explain why two parallel wires carrying current in the same direction attract each other. Two parallel wires 2.5 m apart experience a mutual force of 5 × 10⁻⁵ N per metre; if one carries 0.5 A, find the current in the other, and state whether the two currents are equal. [1+2+2]

Electromagnetic Induction

  1. State Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction. A bicycle spoke rotates within a magnetic field, oriented perpendicular to the field — determine the induced emf this produces. A plane with a 40 m wingspan flies due north at 500 m/s through the Earth's vertical magnetic field component of 5 × 10⁻⁴ T; find the voltage induced across its wings. [1+2+2]
  2. Explain the concept of self-induction, and derive the self-inductance of a solenoid with N turns and radius r. A coil with 2 H of self-inductance must maintain a constant self-induced emf of 60 V — describe how this can be achieved. [1+2+2]
  3. Explain mutual induction and its role in transformer operation. If a secondary coil develops 400 V when the current in the primary coil changes from 0 to 5 A over 0.02 s, find the coefficient of mutual inductance between the coils. [1+2+2]
  4. Define electromagnetic induction and express the induced current in a closed N-turn loop whose flux changes from Φ₁ to Φ₂ over a time t. Derive the expression for the emf induced in a straight conductor of length l, moving with velocity v at an angle θ to a uniform magnetic field B. [1+1+3]
  5. State Lenz's law and explain its role in understanding electromagnetic induction. Discuss whether it is consistent with the principle of conservation of energy. [1+2+2]
  6. Explain self-inductance and derive its expression for an ideal solenoid. An air-core solenoid, 0.5 m long with a 2 cm radius and 100 turns per cm — find its self-inductance. [1+2+2]
  7. State Faraday's law and use it to derive the emf induced in a coil rotating in a uniform magnetic field, commenting on the nature of the resulting emf and current. A generator coil measuring 75 cm × 1.5 m must supply a peak voltage of 220 V for the college during a power outage, rotating in a 0.75 T field at 1500 rpm — find the number of turns required. [1+3+1+3]
  8. Explain what a transformer is and how it aids long-distance power transmission. A primary coil carrying 5 A at 200 V transmits power at 85% efficiency, with the secondary coil operating at the same voltage — find the current in the secondary coil. [1+2+2]
  9. Explain how a solenoid stores energy when carrying a time-varying current. Starting from U = ½LI², derive the energy density u = ½μ₀n²I². A solenoid with self-inductance 500 mH and radius 15 mm carries a steady 2 A current — find the stored energy and the energy density. [1+2+2]

Alternating Current (AC)

  1. Define a phasor and explain how it is used to represent alternating current. Explain why the rms value of AC is more practically meaningful in everyday use than its mean value.
  2. Define inductive reactance and explain its role in reducing power consumption in household appliances. A 220 V, 50 Hz AC supply is connected across a 5 Ω heater, and the current is to be limited to 0.2 A by adding an inductor in series — find the required inductance.
  3. For the given phasor diagram of an L-R circuit, determine whether the current leads the voltage, and justify your answer. A radio receiver's inductor is to carry a current amplitude of 250 mA under a voltage amplitude of 3.60 V at 1.60 MHz — find the inductive reactance and self-inductance of the coil. [1+2+2]
  4. Explain electrical resonance and how it is achieved in a series L-C-R circuit, deriving the resonant frequency expression. A radio circuit has a 5 Ω resistor, an 850 pF capacitor, and a 0.25 H inductor — find the tuning frequency, the circuit's power, current at resonance, and the phase angle between current and voltage. [1+2+2+3]
  5. Given an AC emf E = 220 sin(314t), describe the nature of the resulting current and justify why it can flow through a capacitor. Find the peak voltage, and the capacitive reactance this supply produces across a 2 μF capacitor. [1+2+2]
  6. Define wattless current, and explain why an ideal inductor consumes no power despite carrying current. Distinguish between quality factor and power factor. [1+2+2]
  7. Given the impedance of a series L-C-R circuit as Z = √(R² + (XL − XC)²), sketch how impedance varies with frequency, and discuss whether it can ever be zero. For a 220 V rms supply connected to an L-C-R circuit with 50 Ω resistance, find the current at resonance. [1+2+2]

Wave Optics

  1. Explain the concept of a wavefront and describe how a plane wavefront can be produced. Using the wave theory of light, derive the law of refraction. [1+1+3]
  2. Explain what is meant by the wave-particle duality of light. State Huygens' principle of wave propagation and apply it to prove the law of reflection. [1+2+2]
  3. Define coherent light and describe a method to produce it. Given that the phase difference between interfering waves in Young's double-slit experiment is ΔΦ = (2π/λ)Δx, use this to show that bright and dark fringes have equal width. [1+1+3]
  4. Define diffraction of light and explain how the central maximum arises in single-slit diffraction. For a 0.01 mm slit illuminated by 500 nm light, with a screen placed 3 m away, find the width of the central maximum. [1+2+2]
  5. Explain why light diffraction is not commonly noticeable in daily life, unlike sound diffraction. Given that the nth secondary minimum in single-slit diffraction occurs at d sinθ = nλ, find the angular width of the central maximum, and show it is twice the width of the first secondary maximum. [2+3]
  6. Explain what a diffraction grating is and why it can be treated as a case of multiple-slit diffraction. Light of wavelength 5792 Å produces a third-order spectrum at an angle of 60°30' when incident normally on an unknown grating — find the grating element. [1+2+2]
  7. Define the resolving power of an optical instrument and describe the Rayleigh criterion for resolving two objects. Given the James Webb Space Telescope's mirror diameter of 6.5 m and its shortest operating wavelength of 0.6 μm, calculate its resolving power. [1+2+2]
  8. Explain how the use of a polarizer demonstrates that light is a transverse wave. State and explain the principle illustrated by the accompanying diagram (Brewster's law). A beam of unpolarized light striking a glass surface at 55° becomes completely polarized — find the refractive index of the glass and the angle of refraction. [3+3+2]

Radioactivity

  1. Explain how a certain particle behaves like an electron without actually being one. In a radioactive decay experiment, three emitted particles A, B, and C were observed in an electric or magnetic field. Identify particles A, B, and C with reasoning, and explain why particle C showed no deflection. [1+1+2+1]
  2. i. State the law of radioactive decay. [1] ii. Derive the relation N = N₀e−λt from this law, where the symbols carry their usual meanings. [3] iii. From this relation, derive the expression for half-life. [2] iv. A radioactive sample's activity falls from 1200 disintegrations/sec to 120 disintegrations/sec over 10 days — find its decay constant and half-life. [2]
  3. i. Define the curie as a unit of radioactivity. [1] ii. Identify what the accompanying graph represents, and state the equation it satisfies. [1+1] iii. Using the graph, determine the half-life and mean life of the substance. [3] iv. Starting from 5 million particles of this substance, how many will have decayed after 300 seconds? [2]
